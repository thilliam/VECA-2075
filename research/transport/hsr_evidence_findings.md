# HSR evidence findings — working notes

**Status:** evidence extraction in progress. This is not a VECA route proposal.

## 1. Australia has a long, cumulative HSR evidence lineage

Later Commonwealth HSR work references earlier VFT/Speedrail/VHST investigations rather than treating each new study as an entirely new question. For VECA this is important: repeated findings across 1991, 1999, 2001, 2011, 2013 and current HSRA work should be treated as stronger evidence than any single study's preferred alignment.

## 2. Corridor preservation has already been identified as economically material

The 2011 Phase 1 study estimated the land component of an east-coast HSR network at about **$6 billion in 2011 dollars**. It explicitly observed that acquiring or preserving a corridor in the short term could reduce future costs by reducing the need for additional tunnelling as urban areas expand and preferred alignments become unavailable.

**VECA inference:** corridor reservation is not speculative doctrine invented for VECA. Commonwealth HSR analysis has already identified the mechanism and the cost escalation risk.

**Do not infer:** that the 2011 preferred corridor should itself be protected today. VECA must re-test future settlement geography first.

## 3. Metropolitan access dominates some HSR cost segments

The 2013 Phase 2 staging table estimated:

- Sydney–Canberra: 283 km, $23.0b, $81m/km (2012 dollars)
- Canberra–Melbourne: 611 km, $26.9b, $44m/km
- Newcastle–Sydney: 134 km, $18.9b, $141m/km
- Brisbane–Gold Coast: 115 km, $11.0b, $96m/km
- Gold Coast–Newcastle: 606 km, $34.3b, $56m/km

This is a striking result for VECA. The short Newcastle–Sydney stage was estimated at more than three times the per-kilometre cost of Canberra–Melbourne.

**Inference to test:** the dominant economic question may often be *how a very fast network enters a large existing metropolis*, not merely how cheaply long rural track can be constructed.

This strengthens VECA's requirement to optimise door-to-door systems and to test Parramatta / Western Sydney / peripheral or polycentric access rather than presuming historic CBD termini.

## 4. Earlier studies were strongly CBD-demand oriented

The 2011 work concluded that stations closest to CBDs generated the greatest forecast demand and shortlisted Central, Eveleigh, Homebush and Parramatta in Sydney, alongside peripheral station zones.

That is useful evidence about the travel market *under the land-use and employment assumptions used by that study*. It is not evidence that a 2075 network must terminate at Sydney Central.

VECA should retain the demand modelling methodology and station-catchment findings while explicitly stress-testing changed 2075 employment geography, Western Sydney Airport, Parramatta, AI-enabled work decentralisation, and deliberate regional city formation.

## 5. Current HSRA work is already more polycentric than the historical CBD-only caricature

Current HSRA material proposes stations for central Newcastle, Lake Macquarie, the Central Coast, central Sydney, Parramatta and Western Sydney International Airport. The Australian Government has publicly discussed future Newcastle-to-Parramatta and Newcastle-to-Western-Sydney-Airport travel times as part of the broader east-coast concept.

This matters for VECA because current planning already recognises that Western Sydney and the airport may be national network nodes, not merely suburbs reached after Sydney Central.

## 6. The current Newcastle–Sydney development phase is itself a valuable engineering experiment

The Commonwealth has funded a **$659.6 million two-year development phase** intended to make Line 1 construction-ready. Current procurement includes an initial package with about **35 km of twin TBM tunnels** plus an underground station and associated systems.

VECA should track this program because it will generate modern Australian evidence on:

- TBM productivity and cost;
- geotechnical risk;
- station excavation cost;
- urban and constrained-corridor construction;
- planning/approval duration;
- utility and property-interface cost;
- modern Australian labour/productivity conditions;
- rolling-stock and systems procurement;
- cost escalation from business case to investment decision.

This may become more valuable for VECA's infrastructure-cost research than the final route choice itself.

## 7. HSR and regional development have repeatedly been linked — but not guaranteed

The 2017 transport-connectivity inquiry explicitly considered improved connectivity and HSR as tools for rebalancing settlement in south-eastern Australia. The later government response also noted a key caveat from the 2013 study: regional development is not automatic; it depends on aligned regional-development policy, planning around stations, and supporting business investment/tourism patterns.

That caveat aligns closely with VECA doctrine. A fast line alone does not create a successful regional city. The whole package matters: housing, employment, university/research, energy, water, local mobility, services and amenity.

## 8. Cost comparisons need geography, not just technology

The 2013 system estimate was $114b in 2012 dollars for 1,748 km, or an overall reported average of about $65m/km. The enormous range between individual stages demonstrates why VECA should reject generic claims such as "HSR costs $X per kilometre".

Future cost analysis should decompose each candidate corridor into at least:

- open rural surface alignment;
- major earthworks/cuttings;
- floodplain structures;
- viaduct/bridge;
- mountain/tunnel;
- dense urban tunnel;
- stations;
- land/property;
- power/systems;
- depots;
- environmental mitigation;
- client/design/approval costs.

This same decomposition should later be applied to conventional rail, motorway and utility corridors.
