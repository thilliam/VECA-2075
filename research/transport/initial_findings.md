# Initial Transport Findings

## Finding 1 — HSR studies form a reusable evidence lineage

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** Australian east-coast HSR work is not a single 2013 study; later Commonwealth analysis explicitly reviews earlier VFT (1991), Speedrail (1999) and 2001 East Coast VHST demand work, while the current HSRA program is explicitly building on the 2013 Phase 2 study.  
**Implication:** VECA should compare findings across study generations and give extra attention to constraints or opportunities that recur independently across decades.

## Finding 2 — The 2013 study is a major technical corpus, not merely a route proposal

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** The 2013 Phase 2 program analysed approximately 1,748 km of dedicated route between Brisbane, Sydney, Canberra and Melbourne, regional station options, demand, costs, economics, environmental effects and staging. The preferred study alignment was estimated at approximately $114 billion in 2012 dollars.  
**Implication:** VECA should mine the appendices theme-by-theme rather than summarise the report only at headline level.

## Finding 3 — Metropolitan approaches are a recurring cost/engineering issue

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** Historical HSR work identified large tunnelling requirements, especially on Sydney approaches.  
**Inference:** Metropolitan access may materially alter the system economics of CBD-centric HSR and is therefore relevant to VECA's doctrine of optimising door-to-door systems rather than automatically targeting existing CBDs.  
**Caution:** This does not prove that a peripheral Sydney station is preferable; it only identifies a cost/engineering question worth re-testing.

## Finding 4 — Freight rail utilisation data is informative but incomplete

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** National Freight Data Hub freight-train maps provide train count, gross mass and speed by rail segment using locomotive GPS and ARTC schedule/attribute data. The Hub explicitly warns that train-count coverage is representative mainly of the interstate / ARTC network and does not capture all intrastate or private rail freight.  
**Implication:** Use the NFDH data as a strategic interstate-freight intensity layer, not a complete map of rail importance.

## Finding 5 — East-coast airport gravity is already highly concentrated

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** BITRE's 2025 domestic aviation statistics report approximately 25.4 million domestic RPT passenger movements at Sydney, 24.6 million at Melbourne and 17.6 million at Brisbane. Gold Coast recorded about 5.5 million and Canberra about 2.76 million.  
**Inference:** Existing aviation demand is highly concentrated around the three major east-coast capitals, creating substantial inherited gravity that any alternative long-term settlement/transport system must either exploit or deliberately overcome.  
**Caution:** Domestic passenger counts alone do not measure total airport strategic value, international connectivity, freight, available capacity or future airport constraints.

## Finding 6 — Ports need catchment analysis, not just point locations

**Classification:** Evidence  
**Claim:** The National Freight Data Hub provides port-catchment data linking international import/export freight to regions, plus container-movement data linking ports and regions.  
**Implication:** For VECA, ports should be modelled as supply-chain systems with inland catchments and road/rail interfaces rather than simply as coastal infrastructure points.
