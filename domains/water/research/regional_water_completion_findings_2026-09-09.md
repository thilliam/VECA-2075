# Regional water-system completion — Toowoomba, Hunter and New England

**Date:** 9 September 2026  
**Scope:** closes the highest-priority Stage-1 geographic gaps in the functional water-system seed. This is not yet a climate-adjusted secure-yield comparison.

## 1. Toowoomba / Darling Downs — diversified, interconnected, but not unlimited

Toowoomba's bulk system is materially more diverse than a simple local-dam view suggests:

- Cressbrook, Perseverance and Cooby dams;
- a large groundwater bore network contributing roughly 20–30% of supply on Council's current source page;
- the Wivenhoe-to-Cressbrook pipeline, capable of about 14.2 GL/year with an ultimate capacity of 18 GL/year;
- Mt Kynoch and smaller treatment infrastructure;
- ongoing raw-water main, treatment, reservoir and dam-resilience capital works.

Council's Water Vision 2071 is particularly useful because it exposes the boundary of this inheritance. It plans incremental treatment, pumping and allocation increases, but still anticipates that a new water source or major new Wivenhoe infrastructure will be required from the mid-2040s.

The Toowoomba-to-Warwick pipeline also turns the city into part of a broader regional transfer system rather than an isolated municipal supply. Stage 1 extends permanent supply to Greenmount and the four communities of Nobby, Cambooya, Greenmount and Clifton.

**VECA implication:** Toowoomba has strong water-system optionality for a regional centre, but its future capacity is not free. Inter-basin pumping is energy-intensive and long-term growth still triggers major augmentation.

## 2. Lower Hunter — deliberate diversification is already under construction

The Lower Hunter Water Security Plan explicitly treats drought, climate uncertainty, growth and industry as a system problem. Its portfolio combines:

- existing dams, rivers, sandbeds and groundwater;
- conservation/leakage reduction;
- expanded recycled water;
- potential inter-regional connection to Upper Hunter/Lostock-Glennies sources;
- a permanent rainfall-independent desalination source.

Belmont Desalination is no longer a distant plan. Construction is underway, major marine works were completed in August 2026, and the plant is designed to supply up to **30 ML/day**, around **15% of average regional daily demand**.

**VECA implication:** Newcastle/Lower Hunter inherits an increasingly diversified system and a real climate-resilience investment. The desalination addition is a defined supply increment; the Upper Hunter connection remains an option and must not be counted as committed capacity.

## 3. Tamworth — entitlement is not drought-secure yield

Tamworth primarily uses Chaffey Dam/Peel River and Dungowan Dam, with groundwater as an additional source. Council's April 2026 evidence lists large nominal annual entitlements, including:

- Chaffey/Peel: 16.4 GL/year;
- Dungowan: 5.6 GL/year;
- Tamworth groundwater: 1.055 GL/year.

But the same evidence says the city experienced two severe droughts in 20 years and came within roughly 18 months of running out of water. Current capital includes pipeline and raw-water-storage improvements.

**VECA implication:** legal entitlement and average use badly overstate settlement scalability unless drought reliability and future climate are modelled. Tamworth is a strong example of why VECA needs secure yield rather than storage/entitlement totals.

## 4. Armidale / Guyra / Uralla — water is presently a binding growth constraint

Armidale's existing system centres on Malpas Dam, with Guyra storages, Puddledock emergency storage and groundwater support. Council states Malpas has roughly 13 GL current storage and physical expansion capability to about 26 GL.

However, the NSW Government has assessed the system at the highest water-security risk category. Council's staged project now proposes:

**Stage 1**
- restore Oaky River Dam;
- build a roughly 54 km raw-water transfer to Armidale WTP;
- extend potable supply to Uralla.

**Stage 2**
- raise Malpas spillway about 6.5 m;
- upgrade trunk infrastructure.

Oaky is prioritised before Malpas because secure yield depends on catchment behaviour, not storage volume alone. Council says Oaky's catchment refills more reliably than Malpas.

The NEREZ interaction is especially important. February 2026 Council material estimates the full water-security package could leave about **4 GL/year** spare capacity, while peak construction demand for the Armidale–Uralla–Walcha area could exceed four times that amount.

**VECA implication:** New England's energy investment is not automatically settlement-supporting infrastructure. It may create a competing water demand that makes regional water capital more urgent. This is exactly the kind of cross-layer constraint VECA is intended to expose.

## Methodological findings

The completed systems reinforce five rules for later comparison:

1. **Storage is not yield.** Catchment reliability, evaporation, entitlement and transfer constraints matter.
2. **Entitlement is not secure supply.** Tamworth demonstrates this clearly.
3. **Diversification matters.** Hunter desalination and Toowoomba interconnection improve resilience in ways raw storage totals miss.
4. **Transfer capacity has energy and infrastructure cost.** Toowoomba's Wivenhoe supply requires substantial lift.
5. **Future industry can consume apparent growth capacity.** Armidale/NEREZ is a first explicit example.

## Next water step

Stage 1 geographic coverage is now adequate to move from descriptive systems to a normalized water-capability dataset. The next analytical extraction should capture, where evidence permits:

- climate-adjusted secure yield or reliability metric;
- current potable demand and industrial/non-potable demand;
- licensed entitlement separately from observed use;
- treatment capacity;
- transfer capacity;
- rainfall-independent/recycled capacity;
- committed augmentation and timing;
- estimated residual headroom only where the source actually supports it.

Until then, the map should show system components and augmentation state rather than a single `water score`.
