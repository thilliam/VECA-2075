# Newcastle–Sydney HSR — future-system bias, inherited infrastructure and housing logic

**Research date:** 10 September 2026  
**Domain:** transport  
**Purpose:** test whether HSRA's station/benefit logic is genuinely future-system based or materially anchored to today's infrastructure and current NSW growth plans.  
**Status:** evidence-led synthesis. This note distinguishes source evidence from VECA inference and does not recommend a preferred VECA HSR alignment.

## Executive finding

There is now strong evidence that the Newcastle–Sydney HSR business case mixes two different planning logics:

1. **transformational future land-use logic** — HSR is credited with enabling large quantities of additional housing, denser development and population redistribution; and
2. **inherited/current infrastructure logic** — station-locality and precinct assessment explicitly values existing transport connectivity, jobs/services, hospitals, education, current NSW growth precincts and existing infrastructure capacity.

Those two logics are not inherently incompatible, but Infrastructure Australia has already identified a material modelling mismatch between them. IA states that the transport-demand model and land-use model use inconsistent assumptions and spatial coverage, and warns that this affects design, network integration, costing and the representation of benefits/disbenefits.

IA also explicitly says the social-value assessment does **not align with the land-use change assumed by the project**, and that governments need to consider the capacity and cost of social infrastructure required to serve the additional population.

This is directly relevant to station choice. If a station is preferred partly because Gosford already has a hospital, rail interchange, government services and an established centre, while the same business case claims tens of thousands of additional households generated by HSR, the correct question is not merely whether those assets exist today. It is whether the **future social, utility and local-transport system required by the induced population is included in the model and costed consistently across competing station locations**.

## 1. Gosford Hospital illustrates the distinction between site inheritance and future capacity

### Evidence

Gosford Hospital first opened on 26 May 1945. The hospital site is therefore more than 80 years old in 2026.

However, it is misleading to describe the clinical infrastructure as simply an 80-year-old hospital. NSW completed a major **$348 million redevelopment** in 2019–20, including a new 11-storey clinical services building, emergency department, ICU, theatres, maternity, paediatrics, imaging, cardiac services, additional beds and a rooftop helipad.

The Central Coast regional planning framework explicitly treats the Gosford Hospital campus as a current knowledge/innovation anchor.

### VECA interpretation

The relevant HSR planning variable is therefore not `hospital_age = 80 years`.

It should be decomposed into at least:

- hospital **site/location persistence**;
- age and expected remaining life of major buildings;
- current clinical/service capacity;
- expansion potential;
- catchment and access;
- replacement/augmentation cycle over a 50-year HSR horizon;
- additional hospital demand created by HSR-induced population growth;
- whether equivalent future capacity could be provided at another station locality.

A recently redeveloped hospital is a legitimate inherited-capital advantage for Gosford in the near to medium term. It is not proof that Gosford uniquely minimises health-infrastructure cost through 2075.

## 2. Current Central Coast planning is itself infrastructure-first

The NSW Central Coast Regional Plan 2041 uses an explicit **infrastructure-first, place-based** approach. Its implementation framework assesses:

- capacity of existing infrastructure;
- potential take-up of future development;
- extent/type/cost/timing of new infrastructure; and
- cost effectiveness of supplying infrastructure to new homes/jobs.

The plan expects Central Coast population to grow from about 347,500 in 2021 to more than 404,250 by 2041 and identifies approximately 32,550 additional dwellings over the plan horizon. It directs growth toward established and emerging centres including Gosford, Tuggerah, Wyong, Warnervale and Lake Munmorah.

The plan identifies Gosford as the social/economic heart of the region and highlights Gosford Hospital and the University of Newcastle Ourimbah campus as possible knowledge/innovation clusters.

### VECA interpretation

This creates a path-dependency risk in HSRA's MCA.

If HSR station candidates are scored partly against **existing and currently planned NSW infrastructure**, then locations that NSW has already chosen to invest in will naturally score well. Those strong scores can then be used to justify further transformative investment in the same locations.

That is not necessarily wrong: reusing sunk infrastructure can be efficient. But it is not the same question as:

> Where should a 2060–2075 settlement/transport system place hundreds of thousands of additional residents if major infrastructure can itself be relocated, rebuilt or created over that period?

VECA should explicitly distinguish these two optimisation problems.

## 3. The business case's housing claim is much larger than ordinary regional-plan growth

Infrastructure Australia's Stage 3 evaluation reports that HSRA attributes **46,000 additional households** to improved accessibility from HSR.

It also reports a further **52,000 to 104,000 households** arising from a modelled 2.8% reduction in household size as population redistributes across more dwellings.

IA challenges the evidence for that household-size assumption and says it resembles the reduction seen during the COVID-19 period and is not sufficiently justified for the appraisal.

These HSR-attributed housing effects are much larger than simply saying the Central Coast Regional Plan 2041 expects another ~32,550 dwellings across the region.

### VECA implication

If HSR is credited with inducing housing at this scale, then its supporting system should be modelled at the same scale. That includes, at minimum:

- local roads and intersections;
- feeder rail/bus/active transport;
- water and wastewater;
- electricity/distribution capacity;
- schools;
- hospitals and health services;
- emergency services;
- open space/community facilities;
- local employment access;
- station access/parking/shared mobility;
- environmental and flood mitigation.

It is methodologically inconsistent to claim transformation-scale housing benefits while treating major existing social infrastructure merely as free fixed capacity unless remaining capacity and future augmentation costs are demonstrated.

## 4. Infrastructure Australia explicitly flags this problem

IA's evaluation says:

- assumptions underpinning the **transport demand model and land-use models are inconsistent**;
- the transport model covers the Sydney Greater Metropolitan Area whereas the land-use model covers the East Coast;
- they use differing demand assumptions (including variable versus fixed demand), affecting the modelled response;
- this mismatch has implications for **design, network integration, costing and representation of benefits/disbenefits**;
- the social-value assessment does **not align with the land-use change** from the proposal; and
- governments need to consider **social-infrastructure capacity and the costs of additional services**.

IA also notes that removing land-use and wider-economic benefits collapses the BCR to around 0.2 at 4% and 0.1 at 7%.

### VECA interpretation

This is not merely a theoretical critique invented by VECA. The national infrastructure evaluator has already identified the same structural issue: the transformative land-use scenario and the transport/social-infrastructure appraisal are not yet fully integrated.

This should become a first-class research question in any independent reconstruction of the HSRA model.

## 5. Current precinct investment may be reinforcing the station result

### Gosford

NSW is currently accelerating a 283-hectare Gosford City Centre/West Gosford rezoning intended to unlock about **1,900 additional homes**. The government explicitly cites existing investments including the $348m hospital redevelopment and clinical school/research institute as part of the case for further growth.

### Broadmeadow

The Hunter and Central Coast Development Corporation is similarly coordinating a Broadmeadow growth precinct. Current planning enables up to roughly **3,200 homes** and requires further infrastructure planning/funding around flooding, capacity and staging.

### VECA interpretation

Both selected/interpreted HSR station areas are already NSW Government growth/intervention precincts.

That can mean one of two things (or both):

1. HSRA independently selected the objectively strongest places, which NSW also happened to select for growth; or
2. the MCA rewards alignment with existing government plans and investments, making government-selected growth precincts structurally likely to win.

The scoring matrix/weights are required to distinguish these explanations.

## 6. A better future-system test

VECA should eventually replicate station evaluation under two distinct scenarios.

### Scenario A — inherited-system optimisation

Assume most existing major infrastructure remains geographically sticky and score candidates for how efficiently they leverage:

- hospitals;
- schools/universities;
- conventional rail;
- roads;
- water/sewer;
- electricity;
- existing employment centres;
- government-owned land;
- current committed growth precincts.

This is close to how present-day infrastructure planning normally behaves.

### Scenario B — 2075 system optimisation

Treat infrastructure renewal and new construction as endogenous over a 50-year horizon. Score candidates based on:

- sustainable settlement capacity;
- future population allocation;
- whole-system infrastructure cost;
- replace/expand/build-new lifecycle costs;
- future access technology (including shared/autonomous first-last-mile);
- land/environmental constraints;
- network topology and future HSR extension;
- operating efficiency;
- climate/resource resilience;
- economic geography in 2075 rather than current CBD hierarchy.

The difference between Scenario A and Scenario B is itself a useful measure of path dependency.

## 7. Key unresolved evidence targets

1. HSRA/Urbis station-locality MCA matrix: determine exactly how existing social infrastructure, planned development and future land-use uplift were weighted.
2. HSRA land-use model: recover spatial allocation of the claimed 46,000 additional households and the further 52,000–104,000 household-size effect.
3. Social-infrastructure costing: determine whether additional hospitals, schools and other services are included in CBA costs and where.
4. Utility/enabling-infrastructure costing: determine what water/sewer/power/local transport upgrades are assumed for induced housing.
5. Gosford station precinct capacity/take-up model: recover density, dwelling and employment assumptions by year.
6. Broadmeadow station precinct capacity/take-up model: same.
7. Rejected station candidates: compare their scores before and after land-use/economic-uplift criteria.
8. Counterfactual housing location: identify where the same population/housing would locate without HSR and what infrastructure spending occurs in that base case.
9. Infrastructure lifecycle treatment: determine whether existing assets are treated as persistent/free capacity across the evaluation horizon or whether renewal/replacement/augmentation is costed.
10. Updated Development Phase modelling: track whether IA's transport/land-use/social-infrastructure inconsistencies are explicitly corrected before the final investment decision.

## Assurance / limitations

This is a curated synthesis and hypothesis-testing note, not an exhaustive import. Numerical housing claims are preserved as HSRA/proponent modelling outputs and are not treated as observed outcomes. Infrastructure Australia criticisms are treated as independent evaluation evidence. The exact HSRA MCA weights and detailed land-use/social-infrastructure models remain unresolved and must not be inferred as known.
