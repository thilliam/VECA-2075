# Newcastle–Sydney HSR — FOI evidence and underlying assumptions

**Research date:** 10 September 2026  
**Domain:** transport  
**Purpose:** preserve what can be recovered from FOI releases and public review material about the assumptions, review questions and decision logic underpinning the Newcastle–Sydney HSR business case.  
**Status:** curated research synthesis. Separate released fact, published evaluator criticism, and VECA inference.

## Executive finding

The FOI trail confirms that Infrastructure Australia undertook materially deeper scrutiny than appears in the public business-case messaging, but most of the high-value review documents remain exempt or redacted. FOI 2025-04 identifies the exact internal artefacts that exist: an acceptance checklist, a map request for the reference alignment, clarification questions to HSRA, HSRA responses, an internal evaluation workbook, a waterfall-chart spreadsheet, a Commissioner briefing, draft evaluation report, meeting minutes and later clarification questions.

That is important because it proves the detailed challenge process exists even where the detailed scoring and reviewer comments are not public.

The released IA evaluation nevertheless exposes several key assumptions that materially affect the project result:

1. transport-demand and land-use models are inconsistent in spatial coverage and assumptions;
2. the transport model uses post-COVID settings for 2041/2061, while the newer TZP24 travel-zone projections were unavailable and were not incorporated into land-use projections;
3. land-use benefits dominate the monetised case;
4. 46,000 additional households are attributed to accessibility, while 52,000–104,000 additional households depend on a 2.8% fall in average household size that IA says is insufficiently supported;
5. social-infrastructure capacity/cost is not aligned with the land-use scenario;
6. peer-review issues on beneficiary/reference-group definition and maturity of project-specific benefits appear not to have been addressed;
7. construction disruption is omitted from the CBA;
8. some positive benefits such as resilience/reliability/place effects are also omitted;
9. only around 5% of trips switch from cars and generalised time savings are only about 5.8% of monetised benefits;
10. capacity to deliver the forecast housing was not considered in the business case.

These are not VECA criticisms invented after the fact; they are either directly stated by IA or arise from the document inventory disclosed under FOI.

## 1. FOI access status

Infrastructure Australia identified 12 documents relating to its review of the Newcastle–Sydney HSR business case. Two were released, while ten were fully exempt under Cabinet/deliberative provisions.

The schedule identifies the following high-value documents:

- Map request form for IA to map the HSR alignment — 15 Jan 2025
- HSR acceptance confirmation — 23 Jan 2025 — released with minor redactions
- Proposal Evaluation Website Update — 23 Jan 2025 — released
- Stage 3 Evaluation Summary / clarification questions to HSRA — 5 Feb 2025 — exempt
- Stage 3 Evaluation Workbook, draft — 10 Feb 2025 — exempt
- Stage 3 Evaluation Waterfall Chart spreadsheet — 10 Feb 2025 — exempt
- HSRA responses to IA clarification questions — 13 Feb 2025 — exempt

- Draft Stage 3 Evaluation report — 5 May 2025 — exempt
- Draft Accountable Authority minutes — 5 May 2025 — exempt
- Further HSR clarification questions — 13 Jun 2025 — exempt

**Implication:** the detailed reviewer challenge, business-case compliance checklist, and waterfall-style internal analysis are known to exist, but were withheld during the evaluation process. This strongly suggests the most useful unresolved evidence is not absent; it is simply not publicly released.

### FOI 2024-08 / 2024-09 / 2024-14 / 2025-02

IA's disclosure log and later released material confirm multiple rounds of correspondence with HSRA during business-case development:

- 2024-08: correspondence 1 Mar–28 Jun 2024
- 2024-09: correspondence 29 Jun–19 Sep 2024
- 2024-14: correspondence 20 Sep–6 Dec 2024
- 2025-02: re-release of 2024-14 material

The released bundle is primarily about evaluation timing, publication and Senate Estimates preparation rather than technical assumptions. It nevertheless confirms:

- IA continued asking HSRA for additional information in June 2025;

- IA Commissioners approved the evaluation on 23 July 2025;
- fact checking with HSRA continued during August;
- IA's internal/external talking points explicitly describe the evaluation as examining current and forecast population density, inter-regional travel movements, economic/housing development, alignment and station options, design and delivery.

This supports the conclusion that the evaluation was not a superficial compliance review.

## 2. Transport versus land-use model inconsistency

Infrastructure Australia states that assumptions underpinning the transport-demand and land-use models are inconsistent. The transport model covers the Sydney Greater Metropolitan Area while the land-use model covers Australia's East Coast. IA also notes different inputs such as variable-demand versus fixed-demand assumptions.

IA explicitly says those differences can affect:

- design;
- network integration;
- costing;
- representation of benefits and disbenefits in the economic appraisal.

**VECA interpretation:** this is a central model-risk issue. The rail solution can change settlement geography, but the transport model and land-use model were not yet one fully integrated future-state model. That creates a risk that accessibility, induced settlement and network effects are being valued on inconsistent bases.

---

## 3. Post-COVID travel assumptions are asymmetric

IA's footnote on proposal development says the transport-demand modelling captured COVID/post-COVID changes using PTPM settings recommended by Transport for NSW's Advanced Analytics and Insights team for future base cases in 2041 and 2061.

However, IA also says the post-COVID update to travel-zone projections, **TZP24**, was not available for final modelling/sensitivity testing and **was not incorporated into the land-use projections**.

This is an important underlying assumption issue because the project is strongly dependent on future work location, commuting frequency and household location. The transport model has one post-COVID adjustment path while the land-use model remained based on an older projection basis.

Questions for later replication:

- How much of patronage assumes 5-day commuting versus hybrid work?
- Are peak versus discretionary/business trips separately modelled?
- Does reduced CBD commuting weaken Central's advantage relative to other nodes?
- Would a 2075 distributed-employment scenario shift station rankings?

## 4. Housing assumptions dominate the business case

IA says 58% of monetised benefits come from land-use change. Within the published benefit composition:

- more productive land use / housing zoning and density changes: 38.7% of total benefits;
- investment attraction: 12%;
- cheaper housing/rents and related welfare effects: 9.8%;
- generalised rail/road time savings: 5.8%.

IA says the key housing driver is **46,000 additional households due to better accessibility**.

A further **52,000–104,000 households** are attributed to a forecast fall in average household size as population redistributes into more dwellings.

IA specifically criticises the assumed **2.8% reduction in average household size**, saying the evidence is insufficient and comparing the assumed change to the exceptional COVID-era household-size reduction.

**VECA interpretation:** the headline housing benefit has at least two conceptually different components:

1. dwellings/households made feasible by improved accessibility; and
2. more households created from essentially the same population because of an assumed household-size shift.

These should never be presented as if they are one homogeneous 'HSR creates homes' effect.

## 5. Missing alignment between housing growth and social infrastructure

IA states that the social-value assessment does not align with the land-use change from the proposal and calls for further consideration of social-infrastructure capacity and the costs of additional services.

The same evaluation also says **capacity to deliver forecast housing has not been considered in the business case**.

This materially supports VECA's concern that existing infrastructure can be used as an attractive station-selection input while the costs of scaling that infrastructure for a transformed future settlement pattern are not yet fully integrated.

This applies to:

- hospitals and health services;
- schools and tertiary education;
- water and wastewater;
- local roads and feeder transit;
- electricity distribution;
- community infrastructure;
- construction-market capacity.

The correct 2075 comparison is therefore lifecycle inherited-capital advantage plus required augmentation/replacement cost, not simple proximity to today's infrastructure.

---

## 6. Peer-review issues were not fully resolved

IA says methodological issues identified in peer reviews appear not to have been addressed, specifically including:

- definition of the reference group so beneficiaries can be identified correctly;
- distribution of benefits across the country;
- maturity of project-specific benefit assumptions.

The underlying peer-review documents have not yet been recovered publicly.

**Why this matters:** reference-group definition is crucial when much of the benefit is land-use redistribution. A gain to one region can be partly offset by foregone growth elsewhere. Without a clearly defined national reference group, transfers can be mistaken for net benefits.

---

## 7. The downside and upside omissions are asymmetric but both real

IA identifies likely negative omissions:

- construction disruption not estimated;
- incomplete monetisation of greenhouse-gas emissions;
- social-infrastructure capacity/service costs not aligned with induced development.

IA also identifies positive benefits that may be understated or omitted:

- more reliable journeys;
- improved network resilience;
- place-improvement effects;
- some freight-productivity effects on existing lines;
- possible additional business-productivity effects.

VECA should therefore avoid framing the current CBA as simply 'biased upward'. The stronger conclusion is that the current CBA is **immature and highly assumption-sensitive**, with material omissions in both directions, while its positive result depends heavily on land-use and wider-economic effects.

---

## 8. Transport objectives versus observed mode shift

HSRA's published corporate objectives include reducing private-vehicle use and providing a genuine alternative to existing transport.

IA says Stage 1 produces only around **5% switching from cars** and explicitly says this outcome, together with housing-uplift risk, challenges the objectives of the national HSR project and requires further substantiation that Newcastle–Sydney should be delivered first.

This is particularly important for VECA because it shows that even IA does not accept 'Newcastle–Sydney must obviously be first' as proven by the current evidence.

---

## 9. Option selection still appears path-dependent on current policy

IA's strategic-alignment assessment notes that the proposal aligns with current NSW policies/plans including:

- State Infrastructure Strategy 2022–2042;
- Future Transport Strategy 2056;
- Greater Sydney Region Plan;
- Hunter Regional Plan;
- Net Zero Plan Stage 1;
- Transport Oriented Development Program.

Urbis states that its land-use capacity and take-up work fed population/employment modelling and the station-shortlisting MCA, with explicit assessment of land-use change and economic-uplift potential by option.

**VECA interpretation:** this means current government growth plans likely enter twice in practice:

1. as evidence that some locations are strategically aligned and easier to deliver; and
2. as a basis for modelling future take-up/uplift around those locations.

That may be entirely rational for a near-term project business case, but it is not equivalent to asking which settlement/transport configuration would be optimal in 2075 if current plans were allowed to change.

A renewed FOI request after the business case is public and the 2028 investment/procurement decisions are further advanced may face a materially different exemption balance than FOI 2025-04 did while Cabinet consideration and procurement strategy were still live.
