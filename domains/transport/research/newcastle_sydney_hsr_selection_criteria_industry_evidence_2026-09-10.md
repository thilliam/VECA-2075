# Newcastle–Sydney HSR — selection criteria: industry and stakeholder evidence

**Research date:** 10 September 2026  
**Domain:** transport  
**Purpose:** supplement the HSRA/Infrastructure Australia decision-framework note with evidence from appointed advisers, industry engagement material, industry bodies and stakeholder records.  
**Status:** curated synthesis. Contractor self-descriptions are supporting evidence of scope, not independent validation of HSRA outcomes. Forum commentary is treated only as a lead to primary evidence and is not used as decision evidence.

## Executive finding

The industry/adviser record substantially clarifies what sat inside HSRA's option-selection machinery. Station and alignment selection was not a narrow engineering or travel-time exercise. Formal inputs included transport catchment/accessibility, current and future demand, land-use change and economic uplift, integrated station access, property/acquisition implications, technical feasibility, environmental constraints, rail operations, cost, and broader project/network objectives.

A particularly important confirmation comes from Urbis, an HSRA adviser within the Transport, Land Use and Property package. Urbis says it assessed land-use change potential across station precincts and the wider corridor; its capacity/take-up work fed population and employment modelling; and it supplied inputs to the station-shortlisting MCA specifically evaluating **land-use change and economic uplift potential associated with each option**. This confirms that development/uplift was a formal station-selection input, not merely an economic benefit calculated after stations were chosen.

WSP's project material independently describes the advisory scope as including access strategies for active, public and private transport, interchange concepts and station connectivity; land-use/precinct forecasts for housing and jobs; socio-economic access to employment, education and services; environmental constraints; property acquisition strategy and value capture; and transport/land-use inputs to station locations and alignments.

The available evidence still does not disclose the complete MCA weights or station-by-station scorecard. Recovering those remains the highest-value unresolved task.

## 1. Confirmed criteria / workstreams feeding station and alignment selection

### Land use, housing and economic uplift — confirmed formal MCA input

Urbis states that its HSRA work:

- assessed land-use change potential across station precincts and the broader corridor;
- identified land-use uplift attributable to HSR;
- produced land-use capacity and take-up analysis feeding population and employment modelling;
- assessed development potential for integrated station precincts;
- provided inputs to the station-shortlisting MCA evaluating land-use change and economic uplift potential.

This is strong evidence that a station could outperform another partly because it creates a more favourable modelled land-development outcome, even if today's transport catchment is not superior.

### Transport integration and station access — confirmed

WSP describes Transport Integration work including:

- active-transport access;
- public-transport access;
- private-transport access;
- interchange concepts;
- station connectivity;
- socio-economic access improvements to employment, education and services.

Therefore catchment/accessibility should be reconstructed as a multimodal access problem rather than merely distance from existing rail or CBD population.

### Property, corridor reservation and value capture — confirmed

Urbis and WSP both describe property/acquisition work. Urbis identifies corridor reservation, acquisition costs and development/value opportunities; WSP identifies acquisition strategy, cost support and value-capture opportunities. This means station/alignment scoring was exposed to both the cost of acquiring the corridor and the development value enabled around stations.

### Technical/environmental/operational feasibility — confirmed

WSP says its technical package developed strategic alignments and optioneering while considering engineering constraints and operations, and produced indicative tunnel/bridge typologies, station configurations and maintenance-facility concepts for costing. Environmental and sustainability work identified constraints and risks.

Infrastructure Australia's independent evaluation separately identifies strategic technical analysis, preliminary rail operations and strategic costings in the 18-alignment screen.

## 2. Industry engagement influenced option definition before the business case was frozen

HSRA's July 2024 Call for Registrations of Interest shows that industry engagement was explicitly intended to **inform options** and the reference design, not merely prepare suppliers for procurement. It describes two stages:

1. high-level feedback to define the project/product, inform options and shape an initial delivery strategy;
2. targeted feedback on the emerging strategy and other business-case findings to refine/finalise the business case and reference design.

The process included written submissions, round tables and one-on-one meetings. HSRA provided successful registrants access to an electronic data room and stated that submitted feedback could be used in developing the business case.

The ministerial announcement identified themes including optimising city/region-shaping opportunities through **alignment and station locations**, innovative design/delivery, manufacturing, workforce and emerging HSR technology.

This matters for reverse engineering because some design assumptions or option eliminations may trace to confidential industry feedback rather than a published analytical table.

## 3. Who supplied the principal inputs

The 2024 business-case adviser structure helps identify where to search for leaked methodology and conference material:

- EY — economics, funding/financing, demand modelling and economic development strategy;
- Veitch Lister Consulting under the EY package — transport/demand modelling;
- WSP — Transport, Land Use and Property lead and technical advisory roles;
- Urbis — land use, property, planning, development and economics within the WSP team;
- WSP + Arcadis — technical, environment, sustainability and stakeholder engagement;
- Arup — network operations planning;
- KPMG — project controls, costs, schedule and risks;
- GHD — First Nations participation, culture and heritage;
- RPS — business-case management.

Development Phase appointments broaden this set, notably VLC with L.E.K., Choice Research International and Professor Mark Wardman for demand forecasting.

## 4. Demand modelling — further clues

VLC says demand forecasting is being used to inform decisions from **design to staging**, and that its 2026 work moves from feasibility/high-level assessment into model refinement, assurance and application.

The involvement of Choice Research International and Professor Mark Wardman is potentially significant. Choice Research specialises in behavioural/stated-preference research and Wardman is an internationally published transport-economics researcher known for values of time and generalised-cost parameters. This suggests — but does not yet prove — that Development Phase demand work may include stated-preference/behavioural calibration rather than only assignment of fixed observed trips. Treat this as a research lead until the scope documents are obtained.

## 5. Evidence of political/stakeholder pressure on station criteria

### Broadmeadow

Newcastle City Council unanimously resolved in March 2024 to advocate Broadmeadow as the Newcastle HSR station. Its stated rationale maps almost exactly onto the later HSRA decision dimensions:

- planned urban renewal and at least 17,000 future dwellings;
- current/future public-transport connectivity;
- arterial-road access;
- significant NSW Government-owned land;
- Hunter Park/sport and entertainment precinct;
- alignment with local and state strategic plans.

This is not proof Broadmeadow was predetermined; the council motion itself records that the business case was still to determine station locations. But it demonstrates strong institutional advocacy using the same housing, land, interchange and government-policy criteria later embedded in the formal analysis.

### Alternative philosophy — greenfield/value-capture stations

Former MP and previous parliamentary HSR inquiry chair John Alexander publicly criticised central Gosford/Broadmeadow/Central station concepts and advocated more peripheral/greenfield stations to maximise development and value capture. His argument is useful as a genuine alternative objective function: optimise land value creation and financing rather than present-day centrality/interchange.

Committee for the Hunter publicly countered that station decisions need to balance value capture against existing-community connectivity, access to current jobs/services, urban renewal near existing capacity, labour-force access, carbon intensity and job generation.

This stakeholder dispute is highly informative because it exposes the competing values hidden inside the MCA. Depending on the weights assigned to existing-accessibility versus new-development/value-capture, the same evidence can produce different station selections.

## 6. Gosford is now more definite than earlier public wording suggested

By August 2026 the Commonwealth/HSRA AP1 announcement explicitly names **construction of the Central Coast High Speed Rail Station at Gosford** inside the Berowra–Ourimbah package. ARA's August 2026 milestone release repeats that the 35 km tunnelling package includes a new station at Gosford.

Therefore VECA should update the maturity interpretation: Gosford is no longer merely a secondary-source inference from the business case. It is now the named station in an active ECI package. Its exact footprint/design remains developable, but changing the regional station locality away from Gosford would now require a material AP1 scope reset.

## 7. A useful inconsistency in public NSW understanding

NSW Central Coast Budget Estimates on 25 February 2026 recorded the state Minister for the Central Coast discussing Gosford/Tuggerah/Wyong/Ourimbah/Somersby possibilities as though detailed planning would still choose the location. Yet the released federal business case already identified Gosford, and by August HSRA procurement explicitly included Gosford.

This does not prove a planning defect, but it indicates that the distinction between federal preferred/reference station and state/local understanding was not consistently communicated even after business-case release. It is worth tracing the formal Commonwealth–NSW governance/approval process for station location.

## 8. Current reconstructed selection logic

The evidence supports the following approximate hierarchy, but **not yet exact weights**:

1. Does the option meet Network and Project objectives / anchor requirements?
2. What population/jobs/destinations can access it, using integrated transport?
3. What current and future patronage/demand does it support?
4. What housing, employment, precinct-development and wider land-use uplift can it enable?
5. What does it do to HSR operations: route length, running time, stopping pattern, capacity and future network extension?
6. Is it technically constructible, and with what tunnel/bridge/surface solution and risk?
7. What environmental, heritage and First Nations constraints arise?
8. What corridor/property acquisition is required, at what cost, and what value-capture/development opportunity exists?
9. What is the strategic capital cost and delivery risk?
10. How does the resulting option perform in quantitative MCA and rapid economic appraisal?

The key unknown is how items 2–9 were normalised and weighted against one another.

## 10. Source assurance

**Primary/public-authority evidence:** HSRA ROI document; Commonwealth procurement/media releases; Newcastle City Council minutes; NSW Parliament Budget Estimates.  
**Independent evaluation:** Infrastructure Australia Stage 3 evaluation.  
**Contractor/adviser scope evidence:** Urbis, WSP, VLC project descriptions. Reliable for describing what those firms say they were commissioned to do, but not independent validation that methods/results were correct.  
**Industry/stakeholder positions:** ARA, Committee for the Hunter, John Alexander media commentary. Evidence of advocacy and competing decision philosophies, not HSRA decision facts.  
**Forum posts:** research leads only. Not used as authoritative evidence.
